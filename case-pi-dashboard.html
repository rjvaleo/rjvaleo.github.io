The PI dashboard

I run the release train on a tolling modernization program: ten vendor teams, contracted scope, and a launch date that does not move. This is the planning board I built for it. It shows what each increment holds, what it inherits and what it hands on, and it never shows a number it cannot source.

Who it’s forTen workstream leads, the client, program and delivery leadership, the business-analysis and test leads, and the Jira platform owner.

The problem

  • Ten workstreams, each with its own vendor or contractor team, onshore, offshore and in Europe. They planned in ten different ways, and nobody could see what an increment really held.
  • Scope is fixed by contract. The only levers are sequencing and added people.
  • Carry-over was invisible until it hurt.
  • Leadership needed honest go or no-go answers at each increment's entry and exit gates.
  • The limits: Jira Standard tier, no admin rights for me, no service accounts, and a security review for any add-on.

What I did

  • Interviewed every workstream lead before planning, and turned the answers into one script that every lead plans from.
  • Used AI to turn every interview transcript into complete notes the same day, read and corrected line by line, then wrote a planning playbook where every topic ends in a decision and an owner.
  • Rebuilt the cross-workstream Scrum of Scrums, and run a retrospective with all leads at the end of each increment.
  • Built a working prototype of the board with AI agents, test-first, in five weeks: 87 commits, all mine.
  • Showed each increment as what begins there and what it inherits, by workstream and by capability.
  • Declared every metric with the Jira query that will produce it, so the prototype doubles as the build spec for the Jira administrator.
  • Derived the increment from sprint dates, because no increment field existed.
  • Made it run offline from a single file, for locked-down client networks.
  • Produced the first increment's delivery report from it, and ran the two-day close-out roadshow for the client.

What changed

  • Program Increment 1 closed with every committed requirement accepted.
  • Every lead plans from one script and one picture of all five increments, with carry-forward on the chart.
  • The first increment's delivery report and roadshow came from it.
  • The board now runs inside the team's requirements app.
  • The picture above is the board's layout, redrawn with sample numbers. The figures in the text are the real ones.
  • Still open: writing results back to Jira. One field, end to end, is the go or no-go test.

Decisions

  • Interview every lead before planning.Ten vendors plan in ten ways. The interviews produced one script that every lead now plans from.
  • Build the tool when the tool can't be bought.Jira Standard and no admin rights ruled out the usual add-ons. I prototyped the board myself and wrote it as a spec the Jira owner could build from.
  • A gate with no data shows a dash, never green.A green light nobody checked is worse than no light. Out-of-scope requirements are also kept apart from invalid ones, so the unmapped list holds only what is really unmapped.
  • Every number carries its source.Stated in a document, joined, derived, or entered by a person. 1,552 test links are stated; 803 were inferred by the tool and are marked that way.
  • Ambiguity is shown, never guessed.Some requirement numbers appear twice in the source. Those rows are flagged as ambiguous instead of being quietly merged.
  • Machine suggestions stay marked as proposals.Epics the tool derived matched the team's own only 20% of the time. They wear a proposed badge until a person confirms them.
  • Loaded data is never edited in place.Changes go to an append-only journal, and undo adds the reverse change. The journal doubles as the future change set for Jira.
  • Compute outside Jira, store little inside.About 135 authored decisions belong in the system of record. The rest is a cache. The plan also lists what to stop building.

Running the program

  • Seeing the overload before it hit.I mapped every requirement across the five increments and found the second held almost three times the first. That reshaped the sequencing while there was still time.
  • A diagnosis, then a cadence.Measured against SAFe, the program had release-train scaffolding and almost no team-level rhythm. I built the meeting suite, cadence map and agendas to close the gap.
  • One way to measure progress.Agreed with the program lead: report at the program epic, with design and mapping as entry criteria and a passing test as the exit.
  • Design review by feature, not by line.I proposed moving design review from line-by-line requirements to end-to-end features. The client's reviewers adopted it, with one taxonomy across the program.
  • A numbering fix that broke nothing.A structural flaw in the requirement numbering forced dozens of suffixed duplicates. The fix kept every existing number intact and fed the next RTM version.
  • A roadshow built on confidence.Two six-hour days for the client, framed to build confidence rather than pass a gate. I ran the backward plan, run of show and dry runs, and coached every lead, including the SAP systems integrator, to present their own work.
  • A planning week that ends in decisions.From the lead interviews I wrote a playbook: each topic with its pain point, a starter question and two resolutions, and every conversation ending in a recorded decision and an owner. Then we ran four days in two rooms.
  • Workshops and standards people reuse.Retros after each increment, requirement review workshops that close with a decision log, a story-writing workshop and a program-wide guide to epics, features and stories.
  • Read-outs the same day.Every key meeting gets a bottom-line-first read-out with decisions, actions and the matching email, generated from the transcript and checked against the official minutes.
  • Taking over a stalled handover.When the infrastructure owner role turned over, I took the handover with the cloud subcontractor: inventoried a largely unowned board, framed the four decisions the vendor was blocked on, set a date to decide, and chair their stand-up.
  • A Jira space for the whole contract.I specified a space carrying every contract requirement as an issue, with custom types, more than 60 fields, link types and automations, built in a sandbox first. I also monitor every vendor board and run hygiene passes.
  • A month became a week.A large deck used to mean weeks of combing through content. With AI the roadshow deck came together in a couple of days, and in one evening I built about twenty report designs on real data for leadership to choose from. I still read every line and agree with it before it goes out.

How AI was used

  • A rules file sets scope and git limits for every agent session. It records two times an agent overreached, and the rule each one produced.
  • Plans are test-first specs: the test names are part of the deliverable.
  • I check the agents' numbers. A stale worktree had inflated the test count to 682. The real figure was 384, and fixing that came first.
  • Reusable AI skills do the recurring work: a readout from a meeting transcript, and a refresh of the roadshow numbers from delivery exports, reconciling increment figures against program-wide ones.
